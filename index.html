The puzzle

Why this matters

Assam produces a huge share of India's tea — sold in supermarkets and breakfast blends worldwide. On the estates, families live in employer-tied housing, depend on estate clinics, and earn wages that investigators describe as hard to live on. Women do most of the plucking and much unpaid care at home. Tea is celebrated; plantation labor stays invisible in national economic debate.

Behind every celebrated cup lies a stubborn inequality — and this project is built to make that gap visible.

Today on the estates

Laws on paper, life in the garden

Today's organized tea sector is supposed to provide minimum wages, housing, water, and health services on the estate. Yet Oxfam (2019), Saikia & Parida (2026), and worker advocates still document poverty instruments, health burdens among women pluckers, and weak enforcement.

"More law" alone is not the whole story: we also need to ask who enforces standards and who captures value in the supply chain.

Track Your Tea: Where does the money go?

The numbers below come from Tea Board of India auction records (GTAC FY 2024–25), supermarket listings verified in June 2025, and the Assam government wage notification from April 2026. Retail price per kg is calculated from Tesco UK and Walmart US listings. Auction price: ₹227.70/kg average. Worker wage per kg: ₹280/day statutory minimum divided by the standard 26 kg daily quota = ₹10.77/kg. Currency conversions are illustrative (₹106 = £1, ₹84 = $1). The price decomposition is the author's own calculation.

Price breakdown — per kg of tea, in rupees

Every bar in Track Your Tea is calculated from primary inputs below. Brand and retailer slices are residuals — what remains of retail price after worker wage, auction, and processing are subtracted. All figures are per kg of finished tea, in rupees.

  1. Worker wage per kg: ₹280 statutory daily minimum (Assam notification, Apr 2026) ÷ 26 kg standard daily plucking quota = ₹10.77/kg. Fixed across all brands — the plucker does not share in retail markup.
  2. Estate auction price: GTAC average ₹227.70/kg (Tea Board of India, FY 2024–25). Same for every brand in this decomposition.
  3. Retail price per kg: shelf listing converted to ₹/kg — Twinings Tesco UK (June 2025); Tetley Tesco UK 1.25 kg pack; Lipton Walmart US; Vahdam Daily Assam ₹999 / 500g on vahdam.in. Currency: illustrative ₹106 = £1, ₹84 = $1.
  4. Export / processing: author estimates from industry cost structures — Twinings ₹600, Tetley ₹500 (Tata vertical integration), Lipton ₹400, Vahdam domestic processing ₹180.
  5. Brand margin: retail − worker − auction − processing − retailer where retailer is solved last; equivalently brand = residual after known upstream costs minus retailer share. Computed values: Twinings ₹9,500, Tetley ₹3,200, Lipton ₹800, Vahdam ₹1,200.
  6. Retailer / marketplace margin: final residual to reconcile to retail — Twinings Tesco ₹12,662, Tetley ₹3,412, Lipton Walmart ₹612, Vahdam D2C fees ₹379.
  7. Worker share %: (worker wage ÷ retail) × 100 — e.g. Twinings: 10.77 ÷ 23,000 = 0.047%.
  8. Auction share %: (auction ÷ retail) × 100 — e.g. Twinings: 227.7 ÷ 23,000 = 1.0%.
  9. Markup ×: retail ÷ worker wage per kg — e.g. Twinings: 23,000 ÷ 10.77 ≈ 2,135×.

Reform horizon

Policy implications — if the goal were a living wage

None of these are imminent political wins. They sketch what a coherent reform package would require if tea garden workers were actually meant to benefit from seven decades of labor law — drawn from Oxfam (2019), the Plantations Labour Act framework, ETI/CAO findings, and union demands (ACMS ₹351/day).

Enforce the wage that already exists

Independent inspection with penalties tied to estate licenses — not employer self-reporting. ETI/CAO documented wages below statutory minima on certified estates; law without enforcement preserves the ₹167 vs ₹280 gap.

ETI / Sachdev & Satpute; Assam notification Apr 2026

Index wages to inflation and productivity

Statutory minima rose from ₹217 (2021) to ₹280 (2026) but still trail the ₹351/day ACMS demand and a living wage. Automatic indexation to CPI and auction prices would prevent real wages from eroding between rare cabinet hikes.

Assam Chah Mazdoor Sangha; wage timeline above

Buyer-side price sharing

Oxfam proposed that brands and retailers return a defined share of shelf price to estates — transparent pass-through when auction prices rise, not only when NGOs publish exposés. Shifts value capture without waiting for estate-level charity.

Oxfam (2019) — agenda for change

Certification with real wage floors

Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade labels exist but do not require living wages. Mandatory audited wage floors in buyer contracts — with CAO-style grievance routes — would align ESG marketing with payroll reality.

ETI / CAO; Oxfam (2019)

Decouple housing and health from the employer

PLA ties welfare to estate employment, trapping workers geographically. Public provision of housing, water, and clinics — or portable benefits — would reduce captive-labor incentives and strengthen exit options.

Plantations Labour Act, 1951; Saikia & Parida (2026)

Quota and penalty reform

Daily leaf targets that ignore rain, yield, and pregnancy — with proportional wage cuts — make underpayment routine. Quotas indexed to conditions; penalties banned or capped; piece rates with guaranteed daily floors regardless of weather.

Indian Express (2025); simulator model on this site

Freedom of association and collective bargaining

CAO found insufficient evidence of effective union voice on estates supplying global brands. Protected organizing, sector-wide bargaining, and women pluckers' committees would counter buyer and estate asymmetry.

ETI / CAO; Oxfam (2019)

Transparency from cup to garden

Mandatory disclosure of farmgate, auction, and retail prices on packaging — like the Track Your Tea decomposition — so consumers and regulators can see whether a ₹23,000/kg shelf price ever reaches the ₹10.77/kg plucker.

Author calculation; GTAC FY 2024–25; Tesco / Walmart June 2025

The answer to the question

Assam's tea workers are not poor despite the law. The quota system, tied housing, and estate dependency make poverty a structural feature — not a policy failure waiting to be fixed. The law is real. So is the enforcement gap. And the consumer price never reaches the garden.

What this tells us about development

Development economists distinguish between legal formalization and real institutional change. Assam is a clean case study: the formal rules arrived in 1951. Real wages, mobility, and bargaining power did not follow. The bottleneck is not law — it is the estate structure itself, which keeps workers geographically captive, ties welfare to employment, and lets global buyers extract value without accountability. Until buyer-side pressure or credible enforcement changes the cost calculus for brands, legal progress will keep outrunning economic progress. That gap is not unique to tea — it is the central problem of labor institutions in commodity export economies worldwide.

"The leaf is picked. The price is kept. The law is silent."
