The puzzle

Why this matters

Assam produces a huge share of India's tea — sold in supermarkets and breakfast blends worldwide. On the estates, families live in employer-tied housing, depend on estate clinics, and earn wages that investigators describe as hard to live on. Women do most of the plucking and much unpaid care at home. Tea is celebrated; plantation labor stays invisible in national economic debate.

"Tea is celebrated. Plantation labor is often invisible." — The gap this project is trying to close

Today on the estates

Laws on paper, life in the garden

Today's organized tea sector is supposed to provide minimum wages, housing, water, and health services on the estate. Yet Oxfam (2019), Saikia & Parida (2026), and worker advocates still document poverty instruments, health burdens among women pluckers, and weak enforcement.

"More law" alone is not the whole story: we also need to ask who enforces standards and who captures value in the supply chain.

The answer to the question

Assam's tea workers are not poor despite the law. The quota system, tied housing, and estate dependency make poverty a structural feature — not a policy failure waiting to be fixed. The law is real. So is the enforcement gap. And the consumer price never reaches the garden.

The answer: because the quota system, tied housing, and buyer market power make underpayment structurally profitable — not an oversight. Enforcement would cost the brands. So it doesn't happen.

What this tells us about development

Development economists distinguish between legal formalization and real institutional change. Assam is a clean case study: the formal rules arrived in 1951. Real wages, mobility, and bargaining power did not follow. The bottleneck is not law — it is the estate structure itself, which keeps workers geographically captive, ties welfare to employment, and lets global buyers extract value without accountability. Until buyer-side pressure or credible enforcement changes the cost calculus for brands, legal progress will keep outrunning economic progress. That gap is not unique to tea — it is the central problem of labor institutions in commodity export economies worldwide.
