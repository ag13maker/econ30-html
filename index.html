Assam tea gardens · economic research

When the world drinks Assam tea, who actually benefits?

A capstone story about plantation labor, global markets, and inequality that did not end with independence — told from Assam, with room for my own data next.

The puzzle

Why this matters

Assam produces a huge share of India’s tea. You can buy it in supermarkets and breakfast blends around the world. On the estates, many families still live in housing tied to their employer, depend on estate clinics and water systems, and earn wages that investigators and journalists describe as hard to live on — while women do most of the leaf plucking and much unpaid care work at home.

That gap is the puzzle: tea is celebrated; plantation labor is often invisible in national economic debate. Laws and welfare boards exist, yet reports of poor health, weak bargaining power, and uneven enforcement keep appearing. I am from Assam, and I built this site to make that tension legible — not as a reading-list summary, but as a research project I am still growing.

Named tension: legal progress without economic security on the estate — rights on paper meet a plantation system built to keep workers dependent.

Questions I am pursuing (in plain language)

1. Who gets the money?

When tea is sold at a high price in shops or export markets, how much reaches workers and estates versus brands and middlemen?

2. Do protections work in real life?

Plantation labor laws promise housing, health care, and fair treatment — but do outcomes for women workers match those promises?

3. What never left?

Which parts of the colonial plantation system — tied housing, weak voice, gendered plucking — still shape work today?

Terms I use (quick definitions)

Value chain
Everyone who touches tea from garden to cup — workers, estates, processors, brands, retailers — and who keeps profit at each step.
Statutory labor protections
Worker rights written in law (minimum wages, housing, health services on estates) — not the same as what happens day to day.
Governance gaps
When audits, courts, unions, or company policies fail to enforce standards — even after public investigations.

Historical foundations

How the plantation system was built

British-era expansion turned forest land into tea gardens and brought workers — often migrants — under tight estate control. Historians describe penal contracts that made leaving punishable, heavy recruitment of women and children for plucking, and wage gaps justified by calling women’s work “lighter.” Estates were more than farms: they were enclosed worlds of work, housing, and discipline.

Independence and new labor laws changed the formal rules. My question is what stuck: estate-bound life, gendered plucking, and thin bargaining power when global buyers still capture most of the price.

Today on the estates

Laws on paper, life in the garden

Today’s organized tea sector is supposed to provide minimum wages, housing, water, and health services on the estate. Yet investigators, peer-reviewed reviews, and worker advocates still document poverty instruments (like ration cards), reported health burdens among women pluckers, and disputes over whether audits and certifications actually change conditions.

That is why “more law” alone is not the whole story: we also need to ask who enforces standards and who captures value in the supply chain.

Policy directions

Where evidence points (not my final recommendations yet)

Across sources, three threads recur: share consumer price more fairly, enforce plantation law and health obligations, and strengthen worker voice rather than rely on certification alone. Advocacy pieces warn that boycotts can hurt workers if gardens close — pressure on buyers and public institutions is framed as an alternative.

Legal text: Operational implications of the Plantations Labour Act require line-by-line reading of the Tea Board PDF and any case compilation you trust (e.g. Indian Kanoon after manual access)—this site does not interpret sections not yet excerpted.

Evidence snapshot

Patterns that show up across sources

Poverty instruments on estates

Oxfam’s overview states that half of households visited by its researchers held Government of Assam “below poverty line” ration cards, with eligibility described as 5 kg rice per month per family member.

Banerji & Willoughby (2019) — Oxfam overview

Health policy puzzle

Peer-reviewed abstract frames rising reported disease among female laborers alongside statutory health assurances under the Tea Plantation Act of 1951—motivating deeper primary data work.

Saikia & Parida (2026), PMID 40920342

Insight: Low wages are not only “low bargaining power.” Quota-and-penalty rules can actively extract value from vulnerable workers — especially women on piece rates — by designing targets that are hard to meet when rain, yield, or malnutrition slow plucking. The mechanism turns missed targets into employer savings. That is structural, not accidental pity.

Conclusion

Assam’s tea economy is a global success story and a local labor story at once. The puzzle — celebrated commodity, concealed labor regime — is still open.

This site started from real-world stakes in the gardens, not from abstracts. The reading list helped me see the landscape; my capstone still needs my own data and voice to answer the three plain questions on the story page.

Next: build the empirical core in My project, swap illustrative charts for documented numbers, and keep Assam’s workers visible in how we teach Indian economics.
